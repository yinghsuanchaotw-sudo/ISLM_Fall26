Model assumptions & how to read the results

This is a flexible-price, closed-economy teaching model. Nominal money supply Mˢ and expected inflation πᵉ are fixed. Households take lump-sum taxes as given, labor supply slopes upward, and firms hire until w = MPL. Interest-rate feedback into labor supply is omitted, matching the activity’s sequential solution.

Temporary productivity: Aₜ changes, but expected future productivity and autonomous spending do not. The higher or lower current Y causes a movement along the same IS curve. In a richer model, wealth effects could also shift labor supply or spending.

Permanent productivity: higher expected income shifts labor supply left and lowers desired saving at a given Y; higher expected MPK raises investment. The illustration assumes the labor-demand effect dominates for employment, consumption smoothing offsets the output effect on saving at a given r, and investment responds strongly. Consequently, employment, output, and r rise for a permanent increase here. These outcomes, especially r and P, are not universal; changing response strengths can reverse them.

Capital destruction: the example assumes labor and capital are complements, labor supply stays fixed, and firms raise desired investment to rebuild the lost stock. Rebuilding is an explicit assumption about desired future capital, not a necessary consequence of any temporary Kₜ decline.

Government purchases: Gₜ and current lump-sum Tₜ rise together. The loss of household resources shifts labor supply right. Consumption falls by less than G rises, so national saving falls at a given Y and IS shifts right.

Tax cut: G is fixed; borrowing finances the current tax cut. Households do not fully offset it for future taxes and perceive higher resources, raising consumption and leisure. This chosen response shifts labor supply left and IS right. A failure of Ricardian equivalence alone does not determine the labor response.

Price level: P = Mˢ / L(Ȳ, r + πᵉ). Higher Y raises money demand at a given r; higher r lowers it along the money-demand curve. When Y and r both rise (or both fall), the sign of P depends on which effect dominates. The activity states the result for its illustrated response sizes.

Graph consistency: every intersection is calculated from the same illustrative schedules. Curves are drawn without numerical ticks; their distances are not empirical estimates. In the goods market, a Y change shifts the saving schedule, but it does not by itself shift IS. In the money market, a Y change shifts money demand, but it does not by itself shift LM.
